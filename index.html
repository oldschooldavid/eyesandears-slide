👁 Eyes 👂 Ears Reference
Anatomy of the Eye
Structure · Function · Physiology of Vision
Overview
Primary Functions of the Eyes
  • Detecting visual stimuli (photoreception)
  • Conveying information to the brain via the optic nerve (CN II)
  • Maintaining the circadian rhythm via retinal light exposure
  • Allowing eyesight by reacting to visible light
  • Contributing to balance and spatial orientation
  • Allowing light to enter and projecting it onto the retina
  • Cone cells — detect color (daylight / photopic vision)
  • Rod cells — detect low-light contrast (scotopic vision)
Structures of the Eye
🔵
Cornea
Transparent anterior covering; primary refractive surface (provides ~70% of focusing power)
⚪
Sclera
White fibrous outer coat; protective; continuous with cornea at limbus
🟤
Iris
Pigmented diaphragm; controls pupil size (mydriasis/miosis) to regulate light entry
⚫
Pupil
Opening in iris; dilates in low light (CN III), constricts in bright light
🔷
Lens
Biconvex crystalline structure; changes shape for near/far focus (accommodation); site of cataracts
🟡
Retina
Inner neural layer; contains rods (peripheral/night vision) and cones (central/color vision)
🟠
Macula / Fovea
Central retina; highest cone density; responsible for sharp central vision
🔴
Optic Disc
"Blind spot"; where optic nerve exits; no photoreceptors; cup-to-disc ratio monitored in glaucoma
💧
Aqueous Humor
Clear fluid in anterior/posterior chambers; produced by ciliary body; drained via trabecular meshwork; maintains IOP
🫧
Vitreous Humor
Gel-like substance filling posterior segment; maintains eye shape
🩸
Choroid
Vascular middle layer (uvea); supplies nutrition to retinal pigment epithelium
🔗
Ciliary Body
Produces aqueous humor; contains ciliary muscle for lens accommodation (via zonular fibers)
Pathway of Light & Vision
1
Light enters the cornea

Cornea provides majority of refraction (~70% of total focusing power).

2
Passes through pupil & lens

Iris adjusts pupil size. Lens fine-tunes focus via accommodation (ciliary muscle contraction → lens rounds up for near; relaxation → lens flattens for far).

3
Image projected onto retina

Light stimulates rods (dim light, periphery) and cones (color, central). Image is inverted and reversed.

4
Optic nerve transmission

Electrical signals travel via CN II → optic chiasm (nasal fibers cross) → optic tracts → lateral geniculate nucleus (thalamus).

5
Visual cortex processing

Signals reach occipital lobe (primary visual cortex, V1) → processed into perception of shape, color, motion, and depth.

Intraocular Pressure (IOP)
IOP Overview
Normal Range & Clinical Significance

IOP is determined by the balance between aqueous humor production (ciliary body) and drainage (trabecular meshwork → Schlemm's canal). Normal IOP: 10–21 mmHg.

IOP Ranges (mmHg)
Normal (10–21)
Suspect (22–29)
Glaucoma Risk (≥30)
10212940+

Elevated IOP compresses the optic nerve → progressive ganglion cell death → peripheral vision loss → central vision loss if untreated.

💎 NCLEX Clinical Pearl Cranial nerve II (optic nerve) carries visual information. CN III (oculomotor) controls pupil constriction, eyelid elevation, and most extraocular movements. A fixed, dilated pupil indicates CN III compression — a neurological emergency.
📖 Assessment Tip Normal visual acuity is 20/20. The first number is the testing distance (20 feet); the second is the distance a person with normal vision can read that line. 20/200 = legally blind. Always test each eye separately; document which eye is tested.
Cataracts
Lens Opacity · Age-Related · Surgical Management
📖 Definition A cataract is a clouding of the crystalline lens of the eye that is normally transparent. It causes progressive, painless loss of vision. Like looking through a "frosty or fogged-up window." The leading cause of reversible blindness worldwide.
Risk Factors / Etiology
  • Aging (most common; age >55)
  • Diabetes mellitus (osmotic lens swelling)
  • Family history
  • Obesity
  • Hypertension
  • Smoking
  • UV light exposure
  • Eye trauma / prior surgery
  • Long-term corticosteroid use
  • Myotonic dystrophy
  • Congenital (TORCH infections)
Pathophysiology
  • Oxidative damage to lens proteins
  • Lens fibers lose transparency
  • Protein aggregation → opacification
  • Nuclear sclerosis (yellowing/hardening)
  • Cortical spokes (spoke-like opacities)
  • Posterior subcapsular (PSC) — steroids
Symptoms
  • Blurred / hazy vision (painless)
  • Glare / halos around lights
  • Difficulty reading / driving at night
  • Fading or yellowing of colors
  • Double vision in one eye
  • Frequent prescription changes
  • "Second sight" (temporary improvement)
Diagnostics
  • Slit-lamp biomicroscopy (gold standard)
  • Visual acuity testing (Snellen chart)
  • Dilated fundus exam
  • Retinal exam (rule out other cause)
  • Refraction exam
  • Tonometry (IOP)
Non-Surgical Management
  • Updated eyeglass prescription
  • Magnifying lenses
  • Improved home lighting
  • Anti-glare sunglasses / brimmed hat
  • Limit night driving
  • Monitor for worsening
Surgical Treatment
  • Phacoemulsification (most common)
  • Ultrasound breaks up lens
  • Lens aspirated through small incision
  • IOL (intraocular lens) implanted
  • Extracapsular extraction (ECCE)
  • Laser-assisted cataract surgery
Complications
  • Posterior capsule opacification (PCO)
  • Infection (endophthalmitis)
  • Glaucoma
  • Retinal detachment
  • Corneal edema
  • IOL dislocation
Pharmacological Management
Post-Operative Eye Drops
Standard Post-Cataract Surgery Regimen

Steroid Eye Drops

  • Prednisolone acetate
  • Difluprednate (Durezol)
  • Loteprednol etabonate
  • Rimexolone
  • Duration: 2–6 weeks post-op
  • Purpose: Reduce post-op inflammation

Antibiotic Eye Drops

  • Moxifloxacin (Vigamox)
  • Ciprofloxacin
  • Ofloxacin
  • Duration: ~1 week post-op
  • Purpose: Prevent endophthalmitis

NSAIDs

  • Bromfenac (Prolensa)
  • Ketorolac (Acular)
  • Nepafenac (Nevanac)
  • Purpose: Pain, inflammation, prevent cystoid macular edema (CME)
  • Mydriatics: Phenylephrine, homatropine (dilate pupil, improve visualization)
Preventive Pharmacology
Aldose Reductase Inhibitors

In diabetic patients, excess glucose is converted to sorbitol via aldose reductase in the lens. Sorbitol accumulation → osmotic swelling → lens opacity. Aldose reductase inhibitors may help prevent or delay diabetic cataracts by blocking this pathway. (Currently investigational in humans; well-established in animal models.)

Pre & Post-Operative Nursing Care
Pre-Operative Nursing
  • Obtain baseline visual acuity (document both eyes)
  • Administer dilating drops as ordered (phenylephrine, tropicamide)
  • Confirm NPO status (surgery usually under local/MAC anesthesia)
  • Assess for anticoagulant use (warfarin/aspirin may be held)
  • Educate patient: outpatient procedure, local anesthesia, eye patch post-op
  • Confirm allergies — especially latex and iodine
Post-Operative Nursing
  • Apply eye shield/patch — protect from rubbing or bumping
  • Teach correct eye drop administration order: wait 5 minutes between drops
  • HOB elevated 30–45°; sleep on non-operative side
  • No bending below waist, straining, heavy lifting (>10 lbs) for several weeks
  • Avoid rubbing eye — risk of wound dehiscence
  • Report: sudden vision loss, severe pain, increasing redness → endophthalmitis
  • Wear sunglasses outdoors (photosensitivity post-op)
🚨 Safety Alert — Post-Op Endophthalmitis Endophthalmitis is a rare but devastating post-cataract surgical infection. Signs: sudden severe eye pain, vision loss, hypopyon (pus in anterior chamber), pronounced redness. Onset typically 1–7 days post-op. Requires EMERGENT intravitreal antibiotic injection. Teach patient to seek immediate care if these signs develop — delay can result in permanent blindness.
💎 NCLEX Clinical Pearl Eye drop order matters: If multiple drops are prescribed, wait 5 minutes between each drop. Administer drops from least viscous to most viscous. The lower conjunctival sac holds only ~0.3 mL — instilling more than one drop at a time causes overflow, not better absorption. Never touch the dropper tip to the eye or any surface.
💎 NCLEX Clinical Pearl Post-cataract patients should NOT: rub the eye, bend over (↑ IOP), perform Valsalva, lift heavy objects, or wear tight collars. These all increase IOP and risk wound dehiscence. Activity is restricted for 4–6 weeks.
Glaucoma
Elevated IOP · Optic Nerve Damage · Irreversible Vision Loss
📖 Definition Glaucoma is a group of eye conditions that damage the optic nerve, often associated with elevated intraocular pressure (IOP). It is the leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide. Peripheral (side) vision is lost first, and central vision last — by the time symptoms are noticed, significant damage has occurred. Early detection is critical.
Risk Factors
  • High IOP (>21 mmHg)
  • Age >40
  • African, Hispanic, Asian heritage
  • Family history
  • Diabetes mellitus
  • Hypertension
  • Poor blood circulation
  • Thin central corneas
  • Optic nerve thinning
  • Migraine history
  • Long-term steroid use (eye drops)
  • Prior eye injury
Types of Glaucoma
  • Open-angle (primary) — most common, slow/silent, trabecular meshwork dysfunction
  • Angle-closure (acute) — sudden, painful, emergency — iris blocks drainage angle
  • Normal-tension — optic nerve damage despite normal IOP
  • Secondary — due to trauma, steroids, inflammation
  • Congenital — present at birth, improper development
Symptoms — Open Angle
  • Asymptomatic for years (silent thief)
  • Gradual peripheral vision loss
  • Tunnel vision (late)
  • No pain
  • Discovered on routine eye exam
Symptoms — Acute Angle Closure
  • Sudden, severe eye pain
  • Headache, nausea, vomiting
  • Halos around lights
  • Sudden vision blurring
  • Fixed mid-dilated pupil
  • Rock-hard eye on palpation
  • Conjunctival injection
Diagnostics
  • Tonometry — IOP measurement
  • Gonioscopy — inspect drainage angle
  • Ophthalmoscopy — optic nerve exam
  • Visual field testing (perimetry)
  • OCT — optic nerve imaging
  • Pachymetry — corneal thickness
  • Cup-to-disc ratio (>0.6 = suspicious)
Treatment
  • Eye drops (first-line)
  • Laser trabeculoplasty (SLT)
  • Laser iridotomy (angle-closure)
  • Trabeculectomy (surgical)
  • Drainage implants (tubes/shunts)
  • MIGS (minimally invasive glaucoma surgery)
Complications
  • Irreversible blindness
  • Tunnel vision progressing to total loss
  • Optic atrophy
  • Psychological impact / depression
  • Falls / injury (peripheral vision loss)
Glaucoma Eye Drop Medications — Classes
1st Line — Prostaglandin Analogs (PGAs)
Increase Uveoscleral Aqueous Outflow

Drugs: Latanoprost (Xalatan), Travoprost (Travatan Z), Bimatoprost (Lumigan), Tafluprost (Zioptan), Latanoprostene bunod (Vyzulta)

Mechanism: Stimulate prostaglandin FP receptors → increase uveoscleral outflow of aqueous humor → ↓ IOP by 25–35%.

Dosing: Once daily (evening preferred — better IOP lowering).

Key Side Effects: Increased iris pigmentation (permanent), eyelash growth/darkening (hypertrichosis), periorbital fat atrophy, conjunctival hyperemia (redness).

Nursing: Warn patients of cosmetic changes — especially if using only one eye. Apply light pressure to lacrimal sac (punctal occlusion) for 1–2 min post-instillation to ↓ systemic absorption.

Beta-Blockers
Decrease Aqueous Humor Production

Drugs: Timolol (Timoptic), Betaxolol (Betoptic), Levobunolol (Betagan)

Mechanism: Block β-adrenergic receptors on ciliary epithelium → ↓ aqueous production → ↓ IOP by 20–25%.

Key Side Effects (systemic absorption): Bradycardia, hypotension, bronchoconstriction, depression, masking of hypoglycemia, fatigue.

Nursing: CONTRAINDICATED in asthma, COPD, bradycardia, heart block. Betaxolol is cardioselective (β₁ only) — safer in mild lung disease but still use caution. Apply punctal occlusion. Monitor HR and BP.

Alpha-2 Adrenergic Agonists
↓ Production + ↑ Uveoscleral Outflow

Drugs: Brimonidine (Alphagan P), Apraclonidine (Iopidine)

Mechanism: Stimulate α₂ receptors → ↓ aqueous production + ↑ uveoscleral outflow.

Side Effects: Drowsiness, dry mouth, ocular allergy (brimonidine — ~20% allergy rate with long-term use), conjunctival follicles, tachyphylaxis.

Nursing: Contraindicated in children <2 years (CNS depression, apnea). Caution with MAOIs and TCAs.

Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitors (CAIs)
Decrease Aqueous Production

Topical: Dorzolamide (Trusopt), Brinzolamide (Azopt)

Oral: Acetazolamide (Diamox), Methazolamide

Mechanism: Block carbonic anhydrase in ciliary epithelium → ↓ bicarbonate and Na⁺ transport → ↓ aqueous secretion.

Side Effects (oral): Metabolic acidosis, hypokalemia, kidney stones, fatigue, paresthesias. Cross-react with sulfa allergy.

Nursing: Ask about sulfonamide allergy. Encourage hydration (prevent stones). Monitor electrolytes with oral CAIs.

Miotics (Cholinergics)
Increase Trabecular Outflow

Drug: Pilocarpine (Pilopine)

Mechanism: Stimulate muscarinic M3 receptors → ciliary muscle contraction → opens trabecular meshwork → ↑ aqueous outflow.

Side Effects: Miosis (poor night vision), brow ache, induced myopia, retinal detachment risk.

Nursing: Warn about difficulty driving at night. Used for angle-closure emergencies. Less used now due to side effects.

ROCK Inhibitors
Increase Uveoscleral & Trabecular Outflow

Drugs: Netarsudil (Rhopressa), Netarsudil/Latanoprost (Rocklatan)

Mechanism: Inhibit Rho-associated kinase (ROCK) → ↓ actin/myosin contraction in trabecular meshwork → ↑ outflow + ↓ aqueous production.

Side Effects: Conjunctival hyperemia (very common), corneal verticillata, subconjunctival hemorrhage.

Nursing: Newest class; once daily. Warn about redness — usually harmless but concerning to patients.

Side Effects of All Glaucoma Eye Drops
Systemic Side Effects — Know These for NCLEX
  • Stinging / itching sensation (all drops)
  • Red eyes / periocular skin redness
  • Changes in pulse and heartbeat (beta-blockers — bradycardia)
  • Changes in energy level / fatigue (beta-blockers)
  • Changes in breathing — bronchoconstriction (beta-blockers: CAUTION in asthma/COPD)
  • Dry mouth (alpha agonists)
  • Blurred vision (miotics/carbonic anhydrase inhibitors)
  • Eyelash growth / darkening (prostaglandins)
  • Changes in iris/eyelid pigmentation (prostaglandins — permanent iris darkening)
🚨 Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma — MEDICAL EMERGENCY Sudden severe eye pain + headache + nausea/vomiting + fixed mid-dilated pupil + halos around lights + sudden blurred vision = Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma. IOP can exceed 60 mmHg. Treatment must begin within hours to prevent permanent blindness. Intervention: IV acetazolamide, IV mannitol (osmotic), pilocarpine, topical beta-blocker, and emergent laser iridotomy.
💎 NCLEX Clinical Pearl Glaucoma is the "silent thief of sight" — open-angle glaucoma has no symptoms until significant irreversible damage has occurred. Peripheral vision is lost first (arcuate scotoma), then progresses to tunnel vision. Regular eye exams are the only way to diagnose early — a pressure screening alone is NOT sufficient.
💎 NCLEX Clinical Pearl — Punctal Occlusion Teach patients to apply nasolacrimal occlusion (punctal occlusion) after eye drops: press on the inner corner of the closed eye for 1–2 minutes. This reduces nasolacrimal drainage, minimizing systemic absorption — especially critical with beta-blocker eye drops to prevent bradycardia and bronchoconstriction.
⚠ Safety — Atropine & Angle-Closure Risk Anticholinergic drugs (atropine, scopolamine, antihistamines, TCAs) cause pupil dilation → can precipitate acute angle-closure glaucoma in susceptible patients. Always assess for history of glaucoma before administering anticholinergics or anything that causes mydriasis. Use with extreme caution in undiagnosed narrow angles.
Other Eye Disorders
Macular Degeneration · Diabetic Retinopathy · Retinal Detachment · Conjunctivitis
Etiology / Risk Factors
  • Age >55 (most important)
  • Smoking (doubles risk)
  • Family history / genetics (CFH gene)
  • Obesity, hypertension
  • UV exposure
  • Light iris color
  • Female sex
Types
  • Dry (atrophic) — 80–90%; drusen deposits; slow progression; no cure
  • Wet (neovascular) — 10–20%; abnormal new blood vessel growth (CNV); rapid central vision loss; treatable
Symptoms
  • Central vision blurring
  • Metamorphopsia (distorted straight lines)
  • Scotoma (central blind spot)
  • Peripheral vision preserved
  • Colors appear faded
  • Difficulty reading (central vision loss)
Diagnosis
  • Amsler grid (wavy/distorted lines = positive)
  • Dilated fundus exam (drusen)
  • Fluorescein angiography
  • OCT (optical coherence tomography)
Treatment
  • Dry: AREDS2 vitamins (C, E, zinc, lutein, zeaxanthin)
  • Wet: Anti-VEGF injections (ranibizumab/Lucentis, bevacizumab/Avastin, aflibercept/Eylea)
  • Laser photocoagulation (wet)
  • Low vision aids
  • Stop smoking
Pathophysiology
  • Chronic hyperglycemia → microvascular damage
  • Basement membrane thickening
  • Pericyte loss → microaneurysms
  • Vessel leakage → macular edema
  • Retinal ischemia → neovascularization
  • New vessels fragile → vitreous hemorrhage
Classification
  • NPDR (non-proliferative): microaneurysms, hemorrhages, hard exudates
  • PDR (proliferative): neovascularization, vitreous hemorrhage, traction RD
  • DME: diabetic macular edema (any stage)
Symptoms
  • Often asymptomatic (NPDR)
  • Floaters (vitreous hemorrhage)
  • Blurred vision
  • Sudden vision loss (hemorrhage)
  • Dark spots / patches
Treatment
  • Tight glycemic control (HbA1c <7%)
  • BP control (<130/80)
  • Anti-VEGF injections (DME/PDR)
  • Focal laser photocoagulation (DME)
  • Pan-retinal photocoagulation (PRP) for PDR
  • Vitrectomy (vitreous hemorrhage / traction RD)
Causes / Types
  • Rhegmatogenous — retinal tear allows fluid under retina (most common)
  • Tractional — fibrous bands pull retina (diabetic PDR)
  • Exudative — fluid accumulates without tear (inflammation)
  • Risk: myopia, trauma, cataract surgery, age, family history
Classic Symptoms
  • Sudden onset of floaters (shower of floaters)
  • Flashes of light (photopsia)
  • "Curtain" or "shadow" over visual field
  • Painless
  • Progressive visual field loss
Treatment
  • Laser photocoagulation (retinal tears)
  • Cryotherapy
  • Pneumatic retinopexy (gas bubble)
  • Scleral buckle
  • Vitrectomy
  • Bed rest / face-down positioning (after gas bubble)
Conjunctivitis (Pink Eye)
Bacterial Conjunctivitis

Purulent yellow-green discharge, crusting on lashes (eyelids stuck shut in AM), unilateral→bilateral, no itch. Treatment: Topical antibiotics (fluoroquinolone drops). Highly contagious — contact precautions.

Viral Conjunctivitis

Watery/clear discharge, redness, gritty sensation, often associated with URI. Usually follows cold. Treatment: Supportive (cool compresses, artificial tears). Self-limiting. Most contagious type — strict hand hygiene.

Allergic Conjunctivitis

Bilateral, intense itching (hallmark), watery/mucoid discharge, associated with allergic rhinitis, seasonal. Treatment: Topical antihistamines/mast cell stabilizers (ketotifen, olopatadine), avoid allergen.

🚨 Retinal Detachment — Ophthalmic Emergency "Curtain coming down over vision" + shower of floaters + photopsia = retinal detachment until proven otherwise. This is a medical emergency — the retina must be reattached within hours (especially if macula is still on/attached). Do NOT delay — call ophthalmology STAT.
💎 NCLEX Clinical Pearl — Conjunctivitis Key Differentiator Itching = allergic. Purulent discharge = bacterial. Watery/clear with URI = viral. All three can cause redness. The key differentiator for NCLEX is: itching is the hallmark of allergic conjunctivitis.
Anatomy of the Ear
Outer · Middle · Inner Ear · Auditory & Vestibular Pathways
Overview — Three Divisions
Structural Anatomy

Outer Ear

  • Auricle (pinna) — collects sound
  • External auditory canal (EAC) — 2.5 cm, S-shaped
  • Cerumen (earwax) — protective, antimicrobial
  • Tympanic membrane (eardrum) — vibrates in response to sound; boundary between outer and middle ear

Middle Ear

  • Tympanic cavity — air-filled space
  • Ossicles: malleus, incus, stapes — amplify and transmit vibrations
  • Eustachian (auditory) tube — connects to nasopharynx; equalizes pressure; allows drainage
  • Oval window — stapes footplate transmits vibration to inner ear
  • Round window — pressure relief

Inner Ear

  • Cochlea — hearing (organ of Corti contains hair cells)
  • Semicircular canals (3) — rotational balance
  • Utricle & saccule (otolith organs) — linear acceleration/gravity
  • CN VIII (vestibulocochlear) — carries auditory (cochlear) and balance (vestibular) signals to brainstem
  • Endolymph & perilymph — fluid compartments
Sound Transmission Pathway
1
Sound waves collected by auricle

Funneled into the external auditory canal (EAC).

2
Tympanic membrane vibrates

Sound waves cause the eardrum to vibrate at the same frequency as the sound wave.

3
Ossicular chain amplification

Vibrations transmitted through malleus → incus → stapes. The ossicular chain amplifies sound by ~20-fold via lever mechanics and the area ratio of TM to oval window.

4
Oval window → cochlea

Stapes footplate moves oval window → creates fluid waves in cochlear perilymph (scala vestibuli).

5
Basilar membrane displacement

Traveling waves displace basilar membrane — different frequencies activate different locations (tonotopy): high-frequency = base; low-frequency = apex.

6
Hair cell stimulation → CN VIII

Organ of Corti hair cells bend → mechanotransduction → electrical signal → cochlear division of CN VIII → cochlear nucleus → auditory cortex (temporal lobe).

Vestibular (Balance) System
Semicircular Canals
Rotational Acceleration

Three canals oriented in orthogonal planes (horizontal, anterior, posterior) detect angular rotation. Fluid (endolymph) movement bends cupula → stimulates hair cells → signals CN VIII vestibular branch.

Otolith Organs (Utricle & Saccule)
Linear Acceleration & Gravity

Contain calcium carbonate crystals (otoliths/otoconia) sitting on hair cell bundles. Gravity and linear movement shift otoliths → bend hair cells → balance signal. When otoliths are dislodged → BPPV.

💎 NCLEX Clinical Pearl — CN VIII Cranial Nerve VIII (vestibulocochlear / acoustic) has two divisions: cochlear (hearing) and vestibular (balance). Damage causes sensorineural hearing loss AND/OR vertigo. Ototoxic drugs (aminoglycosides, loop diuretics, cisplatin) damage CN VIII. Always assess hearing AND balance together when ototoxicity is suspected.
📖 Eustachian Tube Function The Eustachian tube equalizes pressure between the middle ear and atmosphere and drains middle ear secretions. In children, it is shorter, more horizontal, and floppier → bacteria from the nasopharynx enter more easily → explains higher rate of otitis media in children. Dysfunction is the primary cause of middle ear pathology.
Otitis — Ear Infections
Otitis Externa · Otitis Media · Chronic Suppurative Otitis Media
Etiology
  • Bacterial (Pseudomonas aeruginosa, S. aureus — most common)
  • Fungal (Aspergillus — "otomycosis")
  • Water exposure (swimming, showering)
  • Trauma (cotton swabs, ear buds)
  • Removal of cerumen (protective barrier)
  • Hearing aids / ear plugs
  • Diabetes, immunosuppression
Classic Symptoms
  • Otalgia (ear pain — worse with tragus manipulation)
  • Pruritus (itching)
  • Aural fullness
  • Purulent/serous discharge (otorrhea)
  • Hearing loss (canal edema)
  • Tenderness on auricle movement
Malignant (Necrotizing) OE
  • Severe form in diabetics/immunocompromised
  • Pseudomonas invades surrounding bone
  • Skull base osteomyelitis
  • CN VII palsy (facial nerve)
  • Requires IV antibiotics + surgical debridement
Treatment
  • Topical antibiotic drops (ciprofloxacin + dexamethasone = Ciprodex)
  • Acetic acid drops (VoSoL) — mild/prophylactic
  • Ear wick if canal severely swollen
  • Keep ear dry — no swimming/submersion
  • Oral antibiotics if severe/spreading cellulitis
  • Analgesics (NSAID/acetaminophen)
Etiology / Organisms
  • S. pneumoniae (most common)
  • H. influenzae
  • M. catarrhalis
  • Viral (RSV, rhinovirus) — precede bacterial
  • Risk: children (age 6mo–2yr peak), daycare, secondhand smoke, bottle feeding, pacifier use
  • Eustachian tube dysfunction → fluid accumulation
Symptoms
  • Otalgia (ear pulling in infants)
  • Fever
  • Hearing loss (conductive)
  • Irritability / crying
  • Otorrhea if TM perforated
  • Bulging erythematous TM on otoscopy
  • Decreased TM mobility (pneumatic otoscopy)
Types
  • AOM (acute otitis media) — acute infection, pain, fever
  • OME (otitis media with effusion / "glue ear") — fluid without acute infection; most common cause of hearing loss in children
  • CSOM (chronic suppurative) — persistent discharge through TM perforation >6 weeks
Treatment
  • AOM <2 yrs: amoxicillin 80–90mg/kg/day × 10 days
  • AOM >2 yrs, mild: watchful waiting × 48–72 hr
  • Amoxicillin-clavulanate (Augmentin) if amoxicillin failure
  • Tympanocentesis if no response
  • Tympanostomy tubes (PE tubes) for recurrent OM or OME with hearing loss
Complications
  • Hearing loss (temporary → permanent)
  • TM perforation
  • Mastoiditis (mastoid air cells infected)
  • Meningitis
  • Brain abscess
  • Cholesteatoma
  • Facial nerve palsy

⚠ Mastoiditis — Complication of AOM Mastoiditis presents with post-auricular pain, erythema, swelling, and ear protrusion (pinna pushed forward and down). It is an extension of OM into the mastoid air cells. Requires IV antibiotics and possible mastoidectomy. Do NOT delay treatment — can progress to meningitis, sigmoid sinus thrombosis, or Bezold's abscess.
💎 NCLEX Clinical Pearl When assessing the ear with an otoscope: pull the auricle up and back in adults (≥3 years) and down and back in children (<3 years). This straightens the curved external auditory canal for better visualization of the tympanic membrane. A normal TM is pearly gray, translucent, with a cone of light (light reflex) at the 5 o'clock (right) or 7 o'clock (left) position.
Hearing Loss
Conductive · Sensorineural · Mixed · Presbycusis · Ototoxicity
Conductive Hearing Loss
Sound Transmission Failure (Outer/Middle Ear)

Causes: Cerumen impaction, otitis media (OME/AOM), TM perforation, ossicular chain damage, otosclerosis, foreign body, atresia.

Key Features: Low-frequency loss; air-bone gap on audiogram; patient may speak softly (hears own voice well via bone conduction). Often reversible.

Treatment: Address underlying cause — cerumen removal, PE tubes, ossiculoplasty, stapedectomy (otosclerosis).

Sensorineural Hearing Loss (SNHL)
Hair Cell / CN VIII / Central Damage

Causes: Presbycusis (aging), noise-induced, ototoxic drugs, Ménière's disease, acoustic neuroma, meningitis, congenital.

Key Features: High-frequency loss first; no air-bone gap; difficulty understanding speech (especially in noise). Usually irreversible.

Treatment: Hearing aids, cochlear implants (severe-profound), remove ototoxin, auditory rehabilitation.

Weber & Rinne Tests
Weber Test (512 Hz Tuning Fork — Forehead)
Normal

Sound heard equally in both ears (midline)

Conductive

Sound lateralizes to affected ear (better bone conduction, no masking by background noise)

Presbycusis — Age-Related Hearing Loss
Presbycusis
Most Common Cause of Hearing Loss in Adults Over 65

Pathophysiology: Gradual loss of cochlear hair cells (especially basal turn — high frequency) and spiral ganglion neurons due to cumulative oxidative damage and vascular changes.

Pattern: Bilateral, symmetrical, progressive high-frequency SNHL. Difficulty understanding speech (especially consonants like s, sh, f, th) in noisy environments.

Management:

  • Hearing aids (first-line)
  • Cochlear implant (severe)
  • Communication strategies: face patient, speak clearly (don't shout), reduce background noise
  • Assistive listening devices (TV amplifiers, captioned phones)
  • Annual audiometry screening after age 65
Ototoxic Medications
⚠ Ototoxicity — Drug-Induced Hearing Loss
Drugs That Damage CN VIII / Cochlear Hair Cells

Aminoglycoside Antibiotics

  • Gentamicin (vestibulotoxic)
  • Tobramycin
  • Amikacin (cochleotoxic)
  • Streptomycin (vestibulotoxic)
  • Neomycin (most ototoxic)
  • Mechanism: Free radical damage to outer hair cells

Loop Diuretics

  • Furosemide (Lasix)
  • Ethacrynic acid (most ototoxic)
  • Bumetanide
  • Mechanism: Disrupt stria vascularis ion transport → ↓ endocochlear potential
  • Usually reversible with discontinuation

Salicylates / Quinine / Chemo

  • Aspirin (high dose >8g/day) — reversible tinnitus
  • Quinine
  • Cisplatin (highly ototoxic) — irreversible high-frequency SNHL
  • Carboplatin
  • Vancomycin (synergistic with aminoglycosides)
⚠ Ototoxicity Monitoring Monitor patients on aminoglycosides or loop diuretics for: tinnitus (ringing in ears), hearing loss, and vertigo/balance changes. Check drug levels (aminoglycoside peak/trough). Report any new symptoms immediately — early discontinuation may prevent permanent damage. Audiometric baseline and serial testing recommended for high-risk patients (long-term aminoglycosides, cisplatin chemotherapy).
💎 NCLEX Clinical Pearl — Ototoxicity Mnemonic "ALAN" — Aminoglycosides, Loop diuretics, Aspirin (high-dose), Noise = main causes of SNHL/ototoxicity. When a patient on furosemide + gentamicin reports tinnitus → synergistic ototoxicity → notify provider STAT. Never administer two ototoxic drugs together without careful monitoring.
Ménière's Disease & Vestibular Disorders
Endolymphatic Hydrops · Vertigo · BPPV · Labyrinthitis
📖 Definition Ménière's disease is a disorder of the inner ear characterized by episodic vertigo, unilateral low-frequency sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus, and aural fullness — the classic "tetrad." It results from endolymphatic hydrops (excess endolymph fluid) in the labyrinth, disrupting hair cell function.
Pathophysiology
  • Excess endolymph accumulation (hydrops)
  • Increased endolymphatic pressure
  • Reissner's membrane distension
  • Periodic membrane rupture → endolymph/perilymph mixing
  • Potassium-rich endolymph bathes CN VIII → attacks
  • Heals → cycle repeats
Classic Tetrad
  • 1. Episodic vertigo — lasting 20 min–12 hr, severe, rotational
  • 2. Unilateral tinnitus — roaring/buzzing, low-pitched
  • 3. Fluctuating SNHL — low-frequency initially
  • 4. Aural fullness — pressure sensation in affected ear
Triggers
  • High-sodium diet (↑ endolymph)
  • Caffeine
  • Alcohol
  • Stress
  • Fatigue
  • Hormonal fluctuations
  • Allergies
Diagnosis
  • Clinical diagnosis (AAO-HNS criteria)
  • Audiogram — low-frequency SNHL (early)
  • Electrocochleography (ECoG)
  • MRI — rule out acoustic neuroma
  • Vestibular function tests (VNG)
  • Glycerol dehydration test
Medical Treatment
  • Low-sodium diet (<1500–2000 mg/day)
  • Thiazide diuretics (HCTZ) or triamterene — reduce endolymph
  • Vestibular suppressants during attacks (meclizine, diazepam)
  • Antiemetics (promethazine, ondansetron)
  • Intratympanic steroids (dexamethasone)
  • Betahistine (widely used outside US)
Surgical / Procedural
  • Intratympanic gentamicin (ablation)
  • Endolymphatic sac decompression/shunt
  • Vestibular nerve section (labyrinthectomy)
  • Reserved for refractory cases
Nursing / Safety
  • Fall prevention — vertigo risk
  • Sit/lie down during attacks
  • Avoid sudden head movements
  • Driving restrictions during active disease
  • Low-sodium diet education
  • Stress reduction techniques
BPPV — Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo
Most Common Cause of Vertigo
Displaced Otoconia in Semicircular Canals

Pathophysiology: Calcium carbonate crystals (otoconia/otoliths) dislodge from utricle macula and migrate into semicircular canals (usually posterior canal). Head movement causes crystals to move → inappropriate endolymph flow → brief rotational vertigo.

Symptoms: Brief (<1 minute) episodes of intense vertigo triggered by specific head positions (lying down, rolling over, looking up). No hearing loss or tinnitus (distinguishes from Ménière's).

Diagnosis: Dix-Hallpike test → positive if vertigo + rotational nystagmus with a latency period, fatiguability, and reversal on sitting up.

Treatment: Canalith Repositioning Procedure (CRP) — Epley maneuver (most effective for posterior canal BPPV): series of head/body positions to guide crystals out of canal back to utricle. Single treatment effective in ~80% of cases. No medications needed for most patients.

Labyrinthitis vs. Vestibular Neuritis
Labyrinthitis

Inflammation of the entire labyrinth (cochlea + vestibule). Causes: viral (herpes, influenza), bacterial (rare, from OM). Presents with: severe vertigo + sudden SNHL + tinnitus. Usually resolves in weeks but may leave permanent hearing loss.

Vestibular Neuritis

Inflammation of vestibular portion of CN VIII only (no cochlear involvement). Causes: viral (post-URI, herpes). Presents with: severe sudden vertigo (days–weeks), nausea, vomiting — NO hearing loss. Treated with vestibular suppressants and corticosteroids.

Tinnitus
Tinnitus — Ringing in the Ears
Subjective vs. Objective Tinnitus

Subjective (most common): Perceived only by patient. Associated with SNHL, noise exposure, ototoxicity, Ménière's, acoustic neuroma. Quality: ringing, buzzing, hissing, roaring. No cure; managed with sound masking, CBT, hearing aids.

Objective: Audible to examiner. Causes: pulsatile tinnitus (arteriovenous malformation, glomus tumor, benign intracranial hypertension), Eustachian tube dysfunction, palatal myoclonus. Requires further workup (MRI/MRA).

🚨 Acute Vertigo — Priority Nursing Interventions Priority: Safety first. Instruct patient to sit or lie down immediately to prevent falls. Keep call light within reach. Raise side rails. Assist with ambulation (major fall risk). Administer vestibular suppressants as ordered (meclizine, diazepam). Provide emesis basin (nausea/vomiting common). Keep environment dim and quiet. Document: onset, duration, triggers, associated symptoms (hearing loss = Ménière's; positional = BPPV; neurological deficits = STROKE — call RAPID RESPONSE).
⚠ Vertigo vs. Stroke — Know the Difference Central vertigo (STROKE) vs. Peripheral vertigo — Key differentiators: Peripheral: severe vertigo, fatigable nystagmus (horizontal/rotational), normal neuro exam, no dysphagia, normal hearing. Central: less intense vertigo, non-fatigable pure vertical nystagmus, neurological deficits (diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, ataxia, weakness). HINTS exam (Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew) can differentiate. Any new acute vertigo with neurological signs = STROKE protocol immediately.
💎 NCLEX Clinical Pearl — Ménière's Diet Low-sodium diet (<2000 mg/day) is the cornerstone of Ménière's lifestyle management. Excess sodium → water retention → ↑ endolymph volume → attacks. Also: avoid caffeine (vasoconstrictive), alcohol, and stress. Teach patients to read food labels and avoid processed/canned foods (high in sodium).
💎 NCLEX Clinical Pearl — BPPV BPPV is the most common cause of vertigo in adults. Key features: brief (<1 min), triggered by head position changes, no hearing loss, no tinnitus. The Epley maneuver is highly effective and is performed in clinic/office. Post-Epley instructions: sleep with head of bed elevated 45° for 24–48 hours; avoid bending over or lying flat to allow crystals to settle.

⚠ CRITICAL Safety — Never in TM Perforation NEVER instill ototoxic solutions into an ear with a suspected or confirmed tympanic membrane (TM) perforation. Aminoglycoside-containing ear drops (e.g., neomycin/polymyxin B) can enter the middle ear and cause permanent SNHL. Always assess for TM integrity first. Safe alternatives: fluoroquinolone drops (ciprofloxacin — not ototoxic through TM).
💎 NCLEX Clinical Pearl — Eye Drop Administration Order When multiple eye drops are ordered: (1) Wait at least 5 minutes between each drop. (2) Instill thinnest (watery) drops before thicker (viscous/gel) preparations. (3) If both eye drops and eye ointments are prescribed, drops first, then ointment. (4) Perform punctal occlusion (nasolacrimal pressure) for 1–2 minutes after each drop — reduces systemic absorption by up to 70%.
💎 NCLEX Clinical Pearl — Glaucoma Drops Priority Beta-blocker eye drops (timolol) have significant systemic absorption and can cause bradycardia, hypotension, and bronchospasm — even from an eye drop. Assess pulse before administration. Teach punctal occlusion. Do NOT administer to patients with asthma, COPD, heart block, or significant bradycardia (<60 bpm). Always perform punctal occlusion after instillation.
