👁️ Eyes, Glaucoma & Cataracts

⚡ Quick Hits

  • Glaucoma = ↑ IOP → optic nerve damage → 2 types: open-angle (gradual) vs closed-angle (EMERGENCY)
  • Normal IOP: 10–21 mmHg — measured by tonometry
  • Cataracts = cloudy lens (SUNDABS mnemonic) — surgery is the ONLY cure
  • Avoid anticholinergics in glaucoma — they ↑ IOP (atropine, ipratropium, diphenhydramine)
  • Post-glaucoma surgery: NO coughing, bending, lifting, vomiting, Valsalva — constipation is priority
🔺 Glaucoma — Pathophysiology & Types
🔥 HIGH YIELD✓ OK

Definition: Leading cause of blindness — increased IOP resulting from an issue with the optic nerve (cranial nerve II). Body produces aqueous humor that must drain; if drainage fails → IOP rises → nerve damage → irreversible vision loss.

Open-Angle Glaucoma (most common): Trabecular meshwork gradual clogging → ↓ outflow of aqueous humor → mild pain + gradual peripheral vision loss (tunnel vision). Develops over time ("Over time, Over angle").

Closed-Angle Glaucoma (emergency): Complete closure of drainage angle → sudden extreme IOP spike. Keywords: EXTREME, "severe," "sudden" eye pain. NCLEX priority = LIFE & LIMB — contact HCP immediately.

🟢 NCLEX Tip — Closed Angle
Memory trick: "Closed angle = Close a door" — sudden closure. Sudden severe eye pain + N/V + halos = EMERGENCY. Notify HCP immediately.
Mnemonic — Open vs Closed
Open = Over time (gradual tunnel vision) · Closed = EXAM PRIORITY (sudden, extreme pain)
Glaucoma
Pathophysiology ▾
  • ↑ IOP from drainage failure
  • Aqueous humor accumulates
  • Optic nerve compressed
  • Open: gradual trabecular clog
  • Closed: acute angle occlusion
Signs & Symptoms ▾
  • Open: mild pain, tunnel vision
  • Closed: sudden severe pain
  • N/V (closed-angle)
  • Halos around lights
  • Blurred vision, redness
Diagnostics ▾
  • Tonometry (IOP 10–21 normal)
  • Ophthalmoscopy (optic nerve)
  • Visual field test (perimetry)
  • Gonioscopy (drainage angle)
  • Slit-lamp exam
Medications ▾
  • Timolol (beta-blocker)
  • Mannitol (osmotic, closed)
  • Prostaglandins (latanoprost)
  • Alpha-agonists (brimonidine)
  • CAIs (dorzolamide)
Nursing Interventions ▾
  • Drop technique (conjunctival sac)
  • Inner canthus pressure post-drop
  • Monitor IOP response
  • Post-op activity restrictions
  • Compliance counseling
Complications ▾
  • Irreversible vision loss
  • Blindness if untreated
  • Optic nerve atrophy
Patient Education ▾
  • No coughing/sneezing/bending post-op
  • No lifting heavy objects
  • No Valsalva — constipation priority
  • Avoid anticholinergics forever
  • Lifelong drop compliance
💊 Glaucoma Medications & Contraindications
🔥 HIGH YIELD✓ OK

Beta-Blockers (most tested): Timolol — decreases aqueous humor production. CI: asthma, COPD, heart block. Apply inner canthus pressure to prevent systemic absorption.

Mannitol (osmotic diuretic): Used for acute closed-angle glaucoma and ↓ ICP. IV administration. Assess LOC every hour. SE: edema begins 30–60 min after administration, heart failure risk. Monitor for HF signs.

Prostaglandin Agonists (1st-line open-angle): Latanoprost (Xalatan) — ↑ uveoscleral outflow. Evening dose. SE: permanent brown iris color change, ↑ lash growth.

Alpha-2 Agonists: Brimonidine (Alphagan P) — ↓ production + ↑ outflow. CI: MAOIs, neonates.

CAIs: Dorzolamide (Trusopt) — sulfonamide-related → check sulfa allergy!

Acetazolamide: Oral CAI — chronic open-angle glaucoma. CI: sulfa allergy. Patient teaching: report paresthesia in facial regions to HCP.

🔴 AVOID — Anticholinergics in Glaucoma
Anticholinergics dry out the body and ↑ IOP. NEVER give: Atropine · Benztropine · Ipratropium · Oxybutynin · Diphenhydramine (Benadryl) — all contraindicated in glaucoma.
📋 Prof. Note — Post-op Pressure Avoidance
After glaucoma surgery, NO added pressure: no coughing, sneezing, bending at waist, lifting heavy objects, N/V, Valsalva maneuver (bearing down). CONSTIPATION IS A PRIORITY complication to prevent.
🔍 Cataracts
🔥 HIGH YIELD✓ OK

Definition: Cloudy lens — lens proteins clump together, scattering light. Painless, gradual blur. No pain until advanced.

Mnemonic — SUNDABS Risk Factors
Sun exposure · Uncontrolled diabetes · Nicotine/smoking · Drinking alcohol · Aging + antioxidant-poor diet · Blood glucose mismanagement · Sun hat deficits

Signs & Symptoms: Gradual and painless · Blurry vision · Halos around lights · White or grey pupil · Absent red reflex on ophthalmoscope · Frequent prescription changes · Poor night vision

Treatment: Non-surgical (glasses, magnifiers, better lighting, sunglasses) until vision impacts daily life. Surgery = ONLY cure: Phacoemulsification — ultrasound waves break up lens → shiny intraocular lens (IOL) inserted. Post-op: steroid drops + antibiotic drops + NSAIDs (prevent CME).

Complications:

  • Retinal Detachment: Pain + sudden vision drop + floaters + flashes — EMERGENCY
  • Endophthalmitis: Purulent (pus) discharge — infection — EMERGENCY
🔴 Post-op Alert
Report immediately: sudden severe pain, vision loss, flashes of light (retinal detachment), or purulent discharge (endophthalmitis). No rubbing, no heavy lifting, no swimming post-op.
Cataracts
Pathophysiology ▾
  • Lens protein clumping
  • Scatters light → blurry vision
  • Progressive, painless
  • Age-related most common
Signs & Symptoms ▾
  • Gradual painless blur
  • Halos around lights
  • White/grey pupil
  • Absent red reflex
  • Poor night vision
Diagnostics ▾
  • Slit-lamp examination
  • Visual acuity (Snellen)
  • Ophthalmoscope (absent red reflex)
  • Retinal exam
Nursing Interventions ▾
  • Fall prevention (vision impaired)
  • Post-op drop teaching
  • Activity restrictions
  • No rubbing eyes
  • Eye shield at night
Medications ▾
  • Steroid drops (post-op inflammation)
  • Antibiotic drops (infection prevent)
  • NSAIDs (prevent CME)
Complications ▾
  • Retinal detachment (emergency)
  • Endophthalmitis (purulent DC)
  • CME (cystoid macular edema)
Patient Education ▾
  • Surgery = only cure
  • Report pain/flashes/purulent DC
  • No driving until cleared
  • Sunglasses outdoors
  • Control DM/BP risk factors
🩺 Ophthalmic & Otic Administration Technique
🔥 HIGH YIELD✓ OK

Eye Drop Technique (4 Key Points):

  • 1. Drop medication into the conjunctival sac (lower eyelid pocket)
  • 2. Gently close and rotate the eyes around after administration
  • 3. Apply gentle pressure to inner canthus (nasolacrimal occlusion) 1–2 min → prevents systemic absorption
  • 4. Wait 5 minutes between different eye drops

Ear Drop Technique (4 Key Points):

  • 1. Affected ear UP — tilt head or lay down
  • 2. Straighten ear canal: Adults = pull UP & OUT · Children = pull DOWN & BACK
  • 3. NEVER occlude ear with dropper
  • 4. Keep medication warm at room temperature (cold drops → vestibular reaction: dizziness/vomiting)
🔴 ISMP Safety Alert
Eardrops vs Eyedrops — "otic" vs "optic" confusion. Always verify route before instillation. Cold ear drops → vestibular reaction (dizziness, vomiting). Always warm to room temperature first.
🟢 NCLEX Pearl — Inner Canthus
Applying inner canthus pressure closes the nasolacrimal duct, preventing systemic drug absorption. Critical for beta-blocker eye drops (timolol) — prevents bradycardia and bronchospasm.
👂 Ears, Otic Drugs & Ménière's Disease

⚡ Quick Hits

  • Ménière's disease = fluid collection in the ear → episodic attacks of vertigo, tinnitus, unilateral hearing loss
  • Perforated TM: Cortisporin (neomycin/polymyxin B) CONTRAINDICATED; Ciprodex/ofloxacin SAFE
  • Ménière's: sodium restriction + avoid caffeine, alcohol, smoking
  • Fall precautions are priority for Ménière's patients — vertigo risk
  • Adults ear drops: pinna UP & OUT · Children: DOWN & BACK — always room temperature
🌀 Ménière's Disease
🔥 HIGH YIELD✓ OK

Definition: Fluid collection in the ear (endolymphatic hydrops) — comes and goes with sudden attacks, typically in ONE ear.

Signs & Symptoms (classic triad + more):

  • 🔊 Tinnitus — ringing in the ear
  • 👂 Unilateral hearing loss — one ear affected
  • 🌀 Vertigo — spinning sensation, sudden attacks
  • 🤢 Vomiting during attacks
  • ⬆️ Increased pressure on affected ear (fullness)

Nursing Management:

  • 🚨 Fall precautions — highest priority (vertigo = fall risk)
  • 🧂 Sodium restriction — reduces endolymphatic fluid
  • ☕ Avoid caffeine, alcohol, smoking — aggravate symptoms
  • Rest in quiet, dark room during attacks
  • Low-sodium diet education
Mnemonic — Ménière's Triad
THV: Tinnitus · Hearing loss (unilateral) · Vertigo — all in one ear, episodic, sudden onset
🟢 NCLEX Priority
Falls are the #1 nursing priority in Ménière's. During an attack: have patient sit or lie down immediately. Instruct to call for help before ambulating.
Ménière's Disease
Pathophysiology ▾

  • Call for help before ambulating

🧬 Cancer & Antineoplastic Pharmacology

⚡ Quick Hits

  • Chemotherapy destroys rapidly dividing cells — affects hair, GI lining, bone marrow (pancytopenia)
  • Pancytopenia: neutropenia (↓WBC) + thrombocytopenia (↓platelets) + anemia (↓RBC)
  • Staging: TNM — Tumor size · Node involvement · Metastasis
  • Benign ends in -oma; Malignant ends in -carcinoma or -sarcoma
  • Palliative = improve symptoms/quality of life · Curative = eliminate cancer
🔬 Cancer Biology & Cell Growth
✓ OK

Cellular Reproduction: Asexual — cells divide creating identical copies. Cancer cells = autonomous, unregulated, asexual reproduction requiring only nutrients and oxygen.

Types of Growth (Plasias):

  • Hyperplasia: ↑ number of normal cells — enlarged organ; NOT cancer yet but increases risk
  • Dysplasia: Abnormal cell growth — pre-cancerous; risk factors: smoking, alcohol, chewing tobacco
  • Neoplasia: Abnormal excessive tissue growth — can be benign or malignant

Tumor Classification: Benign = -oma (adenoma, lipoma) · Malignant = -carcinoma or -sarcoma

Cancer Metastasis: Original cell anchors to location with rich blood supply → replicates → when resources depleted, 1–2 cells split off → travel via bloodstream or lymph to new sites (lungs, liver, brain, bone most common).

Cancer Diagnosis: No single test. Requires: history/physical + lab tests + imaging + biopsy (definitive diagnosis).

TNM Staging: T = Tumor size (T0–T4) · N = Lymph Node involvement (N0–N2) · M = Metastasis (M0=none, M1=distant)

🔵 Note — Signs of Cancer
7 warning signs: Weight/appetite loss · Fatigue · Unusual bleeding · Bowel habit changes · Lumps · Coughing blood · Mole changes
💊 Cancer Treatments & Chemotherapy Complications
🔥 HIGH YIELD✓ OK

Treatment Types:

  • Surgery — remove tumor (curative if localized)
  • Radiation — targeted energy to destroy cancer cells; nursing: protect skin markings, warm water only, skin care, pain management
  • Chemotherapy — destroys rapidly dividing cells (cancer + healthy)
  • Immunotherapy/Biologics — boosts immune system to target cancer
  • Targeted Therapy — specific to cancer cell proteins (proteasome inhibitors, HDAC inhibitors, BTK inhibitors)
  • Hormone Therapy — used for hormone-driven cancers (prostate = reduce male hormones; breast = reduce estrogen)

Palliative vs Curative: Palliative = reduce tumor size + improve symptoms + quality of life (does NOT cure). Curative = eliminate cancer completely. Both may use same treatments (chemo, radiation, surgery) but different end goals.

Chemotherapy Complications (Pancytopenia):

  • 🩸 Anemia (↓RBC) — fatigue, pallor
  • 🦠 Neutropenia (↓WBC) — INFECTION RISK (priority concern)
  • 🩹 Thrombocytopenia (↓platelets) — bleeding risk
  • 💇 Hair loss (alopecia) — temporary, may return different texture/color
  • 👄 Mouth sores (mucositis) — GI cell destruction
  • 🚽 N/V/Diarrhea — GI mucosal shedding
  • 🦴 Organ toxicities
  • 🦴 Hypercalcemia — bone marrow involvement
🔴 Priority — Neutropenia
Infection is the #1 concern in chemotherapy patients. Neutropenia = ↓WBC = inability to fight infection. Monitor temp, WBC counts, implement neutropenic precautions.
🔵 Biological Therapies
Immunotherapy (monoclonal antibodies/MABs), Interferon, Colony-Stimulating Factors (CSFs), Angiogenesis inhibitors. Goal: boost immune system to fight cancer with fewer systemic side effects.
🔴 Lymphoma

⚡ Quick Hits

  • Hodgkin's = Reed-Sternberg cells present (biopsy diagnostic) — Epstein-Barr virus cause in 50% cases
  • Non-Hodgkin's = NO Reed-Sternberg cells — harder to treat, slower growing
  • Classic HESI symptoms: fever, weight loss, night sweats, enlarged PAINLESS lymph nodes
  • Increased risk complication: INFECTION (neutropenia from disease + treatment)
  • Treatment: chemotherapy + radiation; targeted therapy for NHL (Bortezomib, Belinostat, Pirtobrutinib)
🔴 Lymphoma — Hodgkin's vs Non-Hodgkin's
🔥 HIGH YIELD✓ OK

Definition: Cancer within the lymphatic system — begins when healthy lymphatic cells change and grow out of control. Involves lymph nodes (abdomen, groin, pelvis, chest, underarms, neck), spleen, thymus, tonsils, bone marrow.

Hodgkin's Lymphoma: Marked by Reed-Sternberg cells (seen on biopsy under microscope). Cause: Epstein-Barr virus in 50% of cases. More responsive to treatment — cells grow rapidly.

Non-Hodgkin's Lymphoma: NO Reed-Sternberg cells. Multiple subtypes. Usually harder to treat — cells grow slower.

Signs & Symptoms (HESI Favorites):

  • 🌡️ Fever (no chills)
  • ⚖️ Weight loss
  • 😓 Night sweats ("changing sheets")
  • 🔵 Enlarged, PAINLESS lymph nodes (axillary, cervical, inguinal)
  • 🦠 Infections (increased risk)
  • 😮‍💨 Breathlessness, weakness, loss of appetite
  • 🤒 Itchiness, swelling of face/neck

Risk Factors: Weakened immune system (HIV, immunosuppressants) · Family history · Specific infections (Epstein-Barr, H. pylori, HIV) · Age (teens/young adults or >55)

📋 HESI Question — Complications
A nurse caring for a patient with Hodgkin's lymphoma knows the patient is at increased risk for: INFECTION — due to compromised immune system and pancytopenia from treatment.

  • Hair loss temporary
🫁 Pancreatic Cancer

⚡ Quick Hits

  • Pancreatic cancer is difficult to diagnose — symptoms are vague until advanced stage
  • Symptoms: jaundice, abdominal pain, weight loss, elevated blood glucose, greasy stools
  • Diagnosis: CA 19-9 blood test + CT scan + biopsy + endoscopic ultrasound
  • Whipple procedure (pancreaticoduodenectomy) — removes head of pancreas + part of SI + gallbladder + bile duct
  • Complications: hypercalcemia, pancytopenia, pleural effusion, respiratory failure
🫁 Pancreatic Cancer
✓ OK

Overview: 9th most common cancer in women; 4th leading cause of cancer death. Difficult to diagnose early — no specific cost-effective screening. Symptoms vague until advanced.

Risk Factors: Smoking (20–30% of cases) · Family history · Diabetes · Diet · Age · Race · Pancreatitis · Obesity

Signs & Symptoms:

  • 🟡 Jaundice (bile duct obstruction)
  • 🤢 Abdominal/back pain and bloating
  • ⚖️ Weight loss (drastic)
  • 🚽 Greasy or light-colored stool (steatorrhea)
  • 🩸 Elevated blood glucose (DM symptoms)
  • 😴 Constant fatigue and weakness
  • 🤮 Nausea, vomiting
  • 🚽 Constipation or diarrhea
  • 💛 Changes in urine color (dark)
  • 😐 Poor appetite

Diagnostics: Medical history + physical exam + blood tests (CA 19-9 tumor marker) + CT scan + endoscopic ultrasound + biopsy + laparoscopy + genetic testing

Treatment: Chemotherapy → radiation → targeted therapy to shrink → then surgery if possible. Whipple procedure (pancreaticoduodenectomy): resects head of pancreas + first part of SI + gallbladder + bile duct. Cannot resect everything (leave some pancreatic tissue).

Complications: Hypercalcemia · Pancytopenia (neutropenia + thrombocytopenia + anemia) · Pleural effusion · Respiratory failure · Death
